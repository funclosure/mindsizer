# The big idea: startups don't start themselves

Paul Graham's 2013 essay *Do Things That Don't Scale* makes one claim: **startups take off because founders make them take off.** Starting a startup and keeping it running are two separate jobs. The first job is manual and tiring, and nobody else will do it.

> Like **hand-cranking an old car engine**: you strain at the crank until it catches, and after that the engine runs by itself.

```plan
claim: Startups take off because founders make them take off. They do not take off just because the product is good.
mechanism: A founder's arm applies force to a crank. The engine (the startup) turns over but does not fire on its own. Only after enough cranking does it catch, and then it runs without the arm. Without the arm, nothing happens, however good the engine is.
device:
  kind: figure
  resting: An old car engine with a crank handle at the front and a founder's hand on it. A tachometer reads near 'dead cold', with the ignition line marked well above the needle. A caption contrasts the myth ('build a better mousetrap and they come') with the reality ('someone has to turn the crank').
aha: The mousetrap myth pictures the engine starting on its own. The picture shows an arm is required.
source: Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off... A good metaphor would be the cranks that car engines had before they got electric starters. Once the engine was going, it would keep going, but there was a separate and laborious process to get it going.
```

# Two separate jobs

Think of a startup's early life in two phases:

- **Starting:** hard, hands-on, unscalable effort by the founders
- **Running:** growth that feeds itself once it catches

A common mistake is expecting the running phase to begin by itself. Somebody has to turn the crank first.

```plan
claim: Starting a startup and keeping it running are two separate jobs. The first one is manual, and it ends at a threshold.
mechanism: Cranking adds RPM. Below the ignition line, RPM decays back to zero as soon as cranking stops. Above it, the engine sustains its own RPM and the crank is no longer needed. The input is the founder's effort. The relation is the threshold. The output is self-sustaining motion, or a fall back to cold.
device:
  kind: instrument
  operate: The reader turns the crank (presses or drags to add turns) and can let go at any moment.
  changes: The tachometer needle rises with each turn. If released below the ignition line, it sinks back to cold. Once past the line, the engine catches, the crank arm drops away, and the needle holds steady in the 'running on its own' zone.
  resting: 'Two labeled zones on the tachometer: ''Starting: your arm'' below the ignition line and ''Running: its own power'' above it. The needle is cold and a ghost trace shows one failed attempt that sank back. This reads alone as: effort below the line is lost if you stop, and effort past it is no longer needed.'
aha: Letting go just short of the line sends the needle all the way back to zero. Crossing it makes the arm unnecessary. These are two different jobs, and the first one doesn't partly count.
source: Once the engine was going, it would keep going, but there was a separate and laborious process to get it going.
```

# What turning the crank looks like

The most common unscalable job is **recruiting users one at a time.** Stripe was solving a problem people urgently had, and the founders still chased users hard. In the "Collison installation," they didn't email a link. They took your laptop and set you up on the spot.

> Like a **street vendor handing out samples** instead of putting up a billboard: one person at a time, face to face.

```plan
claim: Turning the crank mostly means recruiting users one at a time, in person, and finishing the setup for them on the spot.
mechanism: Two paths from the same 'yes, I'll try it'. In the diffident path, the founder sends a link, and the user has to remember, open it and set things up alone, so each step can lose them. In the Collison path, the founder says 'give me your laptop', and the yes becomes an installed user right there. The output is how many yeses become actual users.
device:
  kind: comparison
  resting: 'Two columns from one ''Will you try it?'' / ''Yes''. Left, ''We''ll send you a link'': a path with several gaps where the user can drop off. Right, ''Right then, give me your laptop'': one step straight to a working setup. Stripe is labeled on the right, with a note that even Stripe, solving an urgent problem, didn''t wait.'
aha: Even after the user says yes, the link path still leaves room to lose them. The Collison installation turns the yes straight into a running user.
source: More diffident founders ask "Will you try our beta?" and if the answer is yes, they say "Great, we'll send you a link." But the Collison brothers weren't going to wait. When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say "Right then, give me your laptop" and set them up on the spot.
```

# Why founders skip the crank

Founders have two reasons to avoid it:

1. **Personality:** they're shy, or they'd rather write code than talk to strangers.
2. **Math:** signing up 10 people by hand seems too small to matter.

The second reason is the dangerous one, because it's a mistake about how growth works.

```plan
claim: Founders skip the crank for two reasons, and the more dangerous one is a mistake about math, not about personality.
mechanism: 'Two forces pull the founder''s hand off the crank. One is personality: shyness, plus preferring to write code over facing rejection from strangers. The other is arithmetic: 10 hand-recruited users look too small to matter. The first is a matter of comfort. The second is a factual error, and the next slide corrects it.'
device:
  kind: static
  resting: 'Two numbered reasons. 1, Personality: ''I''d rather write code than talk to strangers.'' 2, Math: ''10 users can''t be how the big ones started.'' Reason 2 is visually flagged as the dangerous one and points forward to the next slide. The tachometer sits cold, with the hand hovering off the crank.'
aha: The reason that sounds most rational, 'the numbers are too small', is the one that's wrong.
source: There are two reasons founders resist going out and recruiting users individually. One is a combination of shyness and laziness... The other reason founders ignore this path is that the absolute numbers seem so small at first.
```

# Compounding: small numbers aren't small

Growing **10% a week** feels slow. Week to week, it compounds:

- Start: **100 users**
- After 1 year: about **14,000**
- After 2 years: about **2 million**

An early user you recruit by hand isn't one user. They're the first unit of a curve that keeps growing.

> Like **planting a single seed**: it looks like nothing until the orchard is there.

```plan
claim: At 10% a week, 100 users become about 14,000 in a year and about 2 million in two. Small early numbers are the start of that curve.
mechanism: Users after w weeks = 100 × (1 + r)^w. The weekly rate r and the elapsed weeks w are the inputs. Each week's gain is a percentage of an ever-larger base, so the increments look trivial at first and then explode. The output is the user count over time.
device:
  kind: instrument
  operate: A weekly growth-rate slider (1% to 10%) and a time scrubber (week 0 to week 104).
  changes: The user curve redraws and a counter shows users at the scrubbed week. Week 1 at 10% reads 110, barely different from 100. Scrubbing to week 52 shows about 14,000 and to week 104 about 2 million. Lowering the rate to 5% collapses the two-year figure to about 16,000.
  resting: 'The curve at 10%/week over 104 weeks, with labeled points: week 0 = 100, week 1 = 110, week 52 ≈ 14,000, week 104 ≈ 2,000,000. The first months look like a flat line hugging the axis. A note says ''10 hand-recruited users = 10% this week.'''
aha: Going from 100 to 110 looks like nothing, and the curve looks flat for months. Then the same 10% produces 2 million. The 10 users you recruit by hand are the first steps of that curve.
data: 'users(w) = 100 × (1+r)^w. At r=10%: w=1 → 110; w=52 → ≈14,200 (source rounds to 14,000); w=104 → ≈2.02M (source: 2 million). At r=5%: w=52 → ≈1,260; w=104 → ≈16,000. At r=1%: w=52 → ≈168; w=104 → ≈281. Source also notes growth has to slow down eventually.'
source: If you have 100 users, you need to get 10 more next week to grow 10% a week... if you keep growing at 10% a week you'll be surprised how big the numbers get. After a year you'll have 14,000 users, and after 2 years you'll have 2 million.
```

# Fragility: the engine can stall

Almost every startup is fragile at the start. Airbnb's founders went door to door in New York, and that work decided whether the company lived. Before growth catches, a startup can easily die.

> Like a **newborn campfire**: blow on it and feed it twigs, or it goes out. Once it's roaring, rain won't stop it.

```plan
claim: Before growth catches, almost every startup is fragile, and a short burst of manual effort can decide whether it lives.
mechanism: There is a state progression near the ignition line. Without effort, RPM drifts down and the engine stalls. With sustained hands-on work (Airbnb going door to door in New York, recruiting hosts and fixing their listings), RPM climbs past the line. Per the source, about 30 days of in-person work made the difference. Once the engine is running, the same knocks no longer stall it.
device:
  kind: sequence
  resting: 'Three stages along the tachometer. (1) Sputtering just below the ignition line, labeled ''fragile''. (2) About 30 days of door-to-door work in New York, needle climbing, with Airbnb rolly bags. (3) Running: ''unstoppable juggernaut''. A faint branch from stage 1 shows the alternative, a stall back to cold. A side note gives the campfire analogy: feed it twigs or it goes out.'
aha: The company now seen as an 'unstoppable juggernaut' was once about 30 days of legwork away from stalling.
data: About 30 days of in-person engagement made the difference between success and failure (source figure; don't invent any others).
source: Airbnb now seems like an unstoppable juggernaut, but early on it was so fragile that about 30 days of going out and engaging in person with users made the difference between success and failure. That initial fragility was not a unique feature of Airbnb. Almost all startups are fragile initially.
```

# Ask the right question

Don't judge an early startup by asking:

> "Is this taking over the world yet?"

Ask this instead:

> "How big could this get **if the founders did the right things?**"

Founders dismiss their own startups too early. Bill Gates nearly did, when he went back to Harvard instead of committing to Microsoft.

```plan
claim: Judge an early startup by how big it could get if the founders did the right things, not by whether it's taking over the world yet.
mechanism: There are two measuring sticks for the same small startup. The wrong one reads the current needle (cold, tiny) and concludes it's nothing, which is judging a larval startup by an established one's standards. The right one projects where the needle could go if the crank is turned. The first leads founders to dismiss their own company, as Gates nearly did when he returned to Harvard.
device:
  kind: comparison
  resting: Side by side, the same cold tachometer with two readings. Left, 'Is this taking over the world?' reads only the current needle, and the verdict is 'no → quit' (Gates back at Harvard). Right, 'How big could this get if the founders did the right things?' shows a dashed projection to the running zone. The newborn-baby line sits as a caption under the left side.
aha: Both questions look at the same needle and reach opposite verdicts. One sees a newborn as useless, and the other sees what it can grow into.
source: The question to ask about an early stage startup is not "is this company taking over the world?" but "how big could this company get if the founders did the right things?" ... Even Bill Gates made that mistake. He returned to Harvard for the fall semester after starting Microsoft.
```

# Powered aircraft, not projectiles

Many founders hope one **big launch** or one **big-company partnership** will send them flying. That rarely works. A startup doesn't coast on a single push. It needs its own engine running all the time.

A launch only has to do one thing: produce a **core group of happy users** whose enthusiasm drives growth after that.

> Like a **cannonball vs. a plane**: the cannonball gets one blast and falls. The plane keeps flying because its engine keeps turning.

```plan
claim: Startups are powered aircraft, not projectiles. No amount of launch velocity replaces an engine that keeps running.
mechanism: 'A projectile gets one impulse (the big launch or a big partnership) and then only decays, coming down whatever the starting speed. A powered aircraft keeps generating thrust: a core of happy users whose enthusiasm keeps driving growth. The inputs are initial impulse versus sustained thrust. The output is whether altitude is held.'
device:
  kind: instrument
  operate: A 'launch size' slider (small launch → huge launch with partnership) and an 'engine' toggle (off/on, where on means a core of happy users).
  changes: With the engine off, a bigger launch makes the arc taller, but it always lands back at zero. With the engine on, even a tiny launch climbs and keeps climbing. The two flight paths draw over the same timeline.
  resting: 'Two paths on one chart: a cannonball arc from a big blast that falls back to the ground, and a plane from a modest takeoff that keeps rising. The cannonball is labeled ''Big Launch / big partnership'' and the plane ''core group of happy users''. The tachometer on the plane reads ''running on its own''.'
aha: Pushing the launch slider to maximum never keeps the cannonball up. Turning the engine on keeps even the smallest launch flying.
source: I occasionally meet founders who seem to believe startups are projectiles rather than powered aircraft... All you need from a launch is some initial core of users. How well you're doing a few months later will depend more on how happy you made those users than how many there were of them.
```

# A warning sign

The hand-crank model gives you a quick test:

**Be suspicious of any growth plan that skips the effort.**

If the plan is "launch big and the users will come," you're treating the startup as a projectile. Ask: *Who is turning the crank?*

```plan
claim: Any growth plan that leaves out the founders' effort should make you suspicious.
mechanism: 'A simple test applied to a plan: find who is turning the crank. If the answer is ''nobody, the launch or partner does it'', the plan treats the startup as a projectile and fails the test. Extraordinary effort, not just an extraordinary first move, is what passes.'
device:
  kind: static
  resting: 'A single bold rule, ''Be suspicious of any growth plan that skips the effort'', followed by one diagnostic question, ''Who is turning the crank?'' Below are two short example plans: ''Launch big and users will come'' (no hand on the crank, marked suspect) and ''We''ll set up every early user ourselves'' (hand on the crank, passes). The crank appears empty next to the suspect plan.'
aha: Once you ask who is turning the crank, the plan with no effort in it stands out right away.
source: It's not enough just to do something extraordinary initially. You have to make an extraordinary effort initially. Any strategy that omits the effort is ipso facto suspect.
```

# Ways to turn the crank

The unscalable work takes different forms:

- **Recruit by hand:** go to your peers, or find your most enthusiastic users and get more like them (Pinterest recruited at a design-bloggers conference)
- **Delight obsessively:** Wufoo sent handwritten thank-you notes
- **Start narrow:** Facebook launched at Harvard only
- **Build it yourself:** Pebble assembled its early units by hand
- **Be the software:** Stripe set up its "instant" merchant accounts manually

```plan
claim: 'The crank takes several forms: recruiting by hand, delighting users, starting narrow, building it yourself and being the software. It''s the same manual push each time.'
mechanism: Each tactic is one way the founder's arm supplies the starting force the system can't supply yet. Recruiting finds users (peers, or more of your most enthusiastic group). Delight makes them happy enough to drive growth. Narrow focus concentrates heat to reach critical mass. Hand assembly avoids factory minimums. Doing tasks manually stands in for automation that doesn't exist yet.
device:
  kind: figure
  resting: 'Five hands on one crank, or five spokes around it. Each is labeled with a tactic, its company and the concrete act: Recruit by hand, Pinterest at a design-bloggers conference. Delight, Wufoo''s handwritten thank-you notes. Start narrow, Facebook at Harvard only. Build it yourself, Pebble assembling the first several hundred watches. Be the software, Stripe''s ''instant'' merchant accounts set up manually behind the scenes. All five feed the same tachometer.'
aha: 'Tactics that look unrelated, a thank-you card, a Harvard-only launch and hand-built watches, are all the same move: a person doing by hand what the system can''t do yet.'
data: 'Pebble: first several hundred watches assembled by hand. Factory minimum order: usually several hundred thousand dollars (source figures).'
source: Ben Silbermann... went to a conference of design bloggers... Wufoo sent each new user a hand-written thank you note... At first it was just for Harvard students... The Pebbles assembled the first several hundred watches themselves... Stripe delivered "instant" merchant accounts... the founders manually signed them up for traditional merchant accounts behind the scenes.
```

# Small is an advantage

Being tiny lets you do things big companies can't, like personal service and close attention. That attention can make up for a buggy, incomplete product, and talking to your users gives you the best feedback you'll ever get.

> Like a **neighborhood café owner who remembers your order**: no chain can match that, however big it gets.

```plan
claim: Being tiny is an advantage. You can give a level of attention no big company can, and that attention makes up for an unfinished product and gets you the best feedback you'll ever have.
mechanism: A user's experience equals the product plus your attention. A big company has a polished product but can't give individual attention. Tim Cook can't send a handwritten note. A small startup has a buggy, incomplete product but can add attention until the overall experience is insanely great. The same close contact sends direct feedback back into the product.
device:
  kind: comparison
  resting: 'Two stacked bars labeled ''experience of being the user''. Big company: a tall ''product'' segment and almost no ''attention'' segment. Your startup: a short, rough ''product'' segment topped with a large ''attention'' segment, reaching as high or higher. A feedback arrow loops from the startup''s users back to its product. The café-owner analogy is a caption.'
aha: The startup's product bar is clearly worse, yet its total experience bar matches or beats the giant's. Attention is the part a big company can't buy.
source: Tim Cook doesn't send you a hand-written note after you buy a laptop. He can't. But you can... It's not the product that should be insanely great, but the experience of being your user... The feedback you get from engaging directly with your earliest users will be the best you ever get.
```

# A startup idea comes in pairs

Graham's takeaway: a startup idea is **two things**:

1. **What you'll build**
2. **The unscalable things you'll do to get it started**

Turn the crank until the engine catches. Done well, those early habits of chasing users and delighting them stay in the company's DNA as it grows.

```plan
claim: 'A startup idea is a pair: what you''ll build plus the unscalable things you''ll do to start it. Done well, the second part becomes permanent company DNA.'
mechanism: The idea is a vector, not a single number. Component 1 is the product (the engine). Component 2 is the unscalable starting work (the crank). Both are needed to get running. Afterward, the crank habits, aggressive acquisition and delighting users, carry on into the larger company rather than being thrown away.
device:
  kind: figure
  resting: 'The engine and crank shown as one unit labeled ''startup idea = (what you''ll build, how you''ll hand-start it)''. The tachometer finally reads ''running on its own''. A strand from the crank runs into the running engine, labeled ''stays in the DNA: aggressive about users, delighting users'', showing the crank habits persist after ignition.'
aha: The crank isn't thrown away once the engine catches. The habits it built keep running inside the company.
source: it might be a good idea to stop thinking of startup ideas as scalars. Instead we should try thinking of them as pairs of what you're going to build, plus the unscalable thing(s) you're going to do initially to get the company going... if you have to work hard to delight users when you only have a handful of them, you'll keep doing it when you have a lot.
```
