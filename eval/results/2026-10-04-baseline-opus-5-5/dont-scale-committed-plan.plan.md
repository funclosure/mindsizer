# Startups Don't Just Take Off

The most natural assumption about a startup is the most misleading one: that if the idea is good, it will grow on its own. It won't. In the beginning, nothing happens unless the founders make it happen — by hand, one push at a time.

> Like a **hand-cranked early car** — the engine won't run until you physically turn the crank. Only once it catches does it start driving itself.

```plan
claim: A startup produces no motion on its own — it turns over only when the founder applies force by hand.
mechanism: 'Input: founder''s manual crank force. Relation: force turns a cold engine over past the threshold where combustion catches. Output: once it catches, the engine self-sustains; remove force before it catches and it stalls back to dark.'
device:
  kind: instrument
  operate: The reader cranks the engine handle (drag/hold to turn it).
  changes: The flywheel rotation and ignition glow build only while cranking; stop early and it winds down and goes dark, catch the threshold and it keeps running on its own.
  resting: A cold, dark engine at rest with an untouched crank handle — visibly stalled, nothing moving, waiting for a push.
aha: Nothing happens no matter how good the engine is until YOU turn the crank — and letting go too soon lets it die.
source: Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off... A good metaphor would be the cranks that car engines had before they got electric starters. Once the engine was going, it would keep going, but there was a separate and laborious process to get it going.
```

# The Trap: Expecting a Self-Running Machine

Founders picture the finished company — users pouring in, systems humming — and assume the early days should look like a smaller version of that.

But a working machine and a machine you're trying to start are two completely different situations. Confusing them is the core mistake:

- You wait for users instead of going to get them.
- You build features instead of building momentum.
- You judge yourself by size instead of by motion.

```plan
claim: A running company and a company you're trying to start are two different situations, and treating the start like a shrunk-down version of the finish is the core mistake.
mechanism: 'Two machine-states set side by side: a self-running machine (momentum already present, coasts) versus a starting machine (no momentum, needs external force). The wrong assumptions transfer from left to right and misfire.'
device:
  kind: comparison
  changes: 'Three founder behaviors flip between the columns: wait for users → go get them; build features → build momentum; judge by size → judge by motion.'
  resting: 'Left: a humming, self-turning machine labeled ''running''. Right: the same machine cold and still labeled ''starting'', with the three mismatched assumptions listed between them.'
source: Founders picture the finished company... But a working machine and a machine you're trying to start are two completely different situations. Confusing them is the core mistake.
```

# The Real Model: You Are the Engine at First

Early on, there is no momentum to coast on. The growth you see is the growth *you personally create* through effort that could never work at large scale. That's not a flaw in your plan — it's the whole job of the starting phase.

> Like **pushing a stalled car** — it feels absurd to shove a machine meant to drive itself, but that shove is exactly what gets it rolling fast enough to start.

```plan
claim: At the start there is no momentum to coast on — the growth you see is growth you personally generate through effort that couldn't work at scale.
mechanism: 'Input: the founder''s own labor substituted in where an automated engine would normally be. Relation: personal effort is the sole source of forward motion. Output: every unit of growth traces directly back to a founder action, not to any self-running system.'
device:
  kind: figure
  resting: The founder drawn in the engine's place — body coupled to the flywheel — with the growth curve emerging directly from their exertion, no automated drivetrain present.
data: 'Airbnb: ~30 days of founders going door-to-door in New York engaging users in person made the difference between success and failure.'
source: Airbnb... early on it was so fragile that about 30 days of going out and engaging in person with users made the difference between success and failure... The growth you see is the growth you personally create through effort that could never work at large scale.
```

# Why It Has to Be Unscalable

"Scalable" tactics assume a force already in motion — a crowd that spreads the word, a brand people already trust. At the start, none of that exists yet.

So the only lever you have is disproportionate personal effort: doing things that clearly wouldn't work if you had a million users, precisely *because* you don't have them yet. The unscalability isn't a compromise — it matches the reality of where you are.

```plan
claim: Scalable tactics assume a force already in motion; at the start none exists, so disproportionate personal effort is the only lever available.
mechanism: 'Two levers acting on the same load: the ''scalable'' lever (word-of-mouth, brand trust) requires pre-existing momentum to transmit any force, so at zero momentum it moves nothing; the ''unscalable'' lever (direct personal effort) supplies force from the founder directly and works precisely because the user count is tiny.'
device:
  kind: comparison
  changes: At zero-momentum the scalable lever is slack and transmits no force; the unscalable lever is taut and moves the load — the difference is whether the lever needs a crowd that doesn't exist yet.
  resting: 'Left: a scalable lever connected to an absent flywheel, hanging useless. Right: an unscalable lever driven straight by the founder''s hand, actually turning the load.'
source: '''Scalable'' tactics assume a force already in motion — a crowd that spreads the word, a brand people already trust. At the start, none of that exists yet... doing things that clearly wouldn''t work if you had a million users, precisely because you don''t have them yet.'
```

# Recruit Users One by One

Don't broadcast and wait. Go find individual people, reach them personally, and set them up yourself. Each early user is won by hand, not attracted by gravity.

> Like **Stripe's founders installing their software on someone's laptop right there in the room** — not "try us sometime," but "give me your computer, I'll do it now."

```plan
claim: Users are won one at a time by hand, and those small numbers compound into enormous ones through sustained weekly growth.
mechanism: 'Input: founder personally reaches and sets up one individual user (not broadcast-and-wait). Relation: each added user compounds at a weekly growth rate. Output: a tiny starting base multiplies over time — the manual act is small, the compounding is not.'
device:
  kind: sequence
  resting: 'A staged progression: one founder handing setup to one user (Collison installation), then the compounding count ticking up week by week along the same ignition arc.'
data: '10% weekly growth: 100 users → 110 next week → ~14,000 after 1 year (52 weeks) → ~2,000,000 after 2 years (104 weeks). Stripe ''Collison installation'': ''Right then, give me your laptop'' — set up on the spot rather than ''we''ll send you a link.'''
source: You have to go out and get them... 'Collison installation'... 'Right then, give me your laptop' and set them up on the spot... If you have 100 users, you need to get 10 more next week to grow 10% a week... After a year you'll have 14,000 users, and after 2 years you'll have 2 million.
```

# Delight Beyond What's Reasonable

With a handful of users, you can afford to treat each one extraordinarily well. An 'insanely great' *experience* matters more than a polished product — your attention can cover for bugs and gaps a bigger company could never get away with.

> Like a **new restaurant where the owner personally greets every table** — the food may be uneven, but the care makes people fall in love and come back.

```plan
claim: With only a handful of users you can give each an insanely great experience, and that attentiveness compensates for a buggy, incomplete product.
mechanism: 'Input: founder attentiveness per user (feasible only at small scale). Relation: total experience quality = product quality + attentiveness; attentiveness fills the gap the incomplete product leaves. Output: users feel delighted even when the product is rough.'
device:
  kind: instrument
  operate: The reader raises an 'attentiveness' dial while the product stays buggy/incomplete.
  changes: As attentiveness rises, the overall experience meter climbs into 'insanely great' even though the product bar stays low — and the dial is only reachable while user count is small.
  resting: A low, gap-filled product bar beside an experience meter sitting low, with the attentiveness dial at its resting minimum — the gap plainly visible and unfilled.
aha: You can deliver an insanely great experience on a bad product — the missing product quality is made up by attention no big company could afford.
data: Wufoo sent each new user a hand-written thank-you note; Steve Jobs' 'insanely great' = quality of execution to a pathological degree. Tim Cook can't send you a note; you can.
source: It's not the product that should be insanely great, but the experience of being your user... You can and should give users an insanely great experience with an early, incomplete, buggy product, if you make up the difference with attentiveness.
```

# Cranking Is Also How You Learn

Doing things by hand isn't just about growth — it's the tightest possible feedback loop. Sitting with early users shows you exactly what's broken, what confuses them, and what they actually want.

The effort that grows the company and the effort that improves the product are the same effort. You can't automate this stage because automating it would blind you to the very lessons you need.

```plan
claim: Doing things by hand is also the tightest feedback loop — the effort that grows the company and the effort that improves the product are the same effort.
mechanism: 'Input: founder over-engages directly with early users. Relation: that single act splits into two returns — it drives growth AND surfaces exactly what''s broken/confusing/wanted. Output: product improves from direct contact; automating this stage would sever the feedback and blind you.'
device:
  kind: figure
  resting: One stream of founder effort forking into two outputs — a growth line and a product-improvement line — with a feedback arrow running from users back into the product; a crossed-out 'automate' shows the loop severed.
source: Over-engaging with early users is not just a permissible technique for getting growth rolling. For most successful startups it's a necessary part of the feedback loop that makes the product good... The feedback you get from engaging directly with your earliest users will be the best you ever get.
```

# Start Narrow to Catch Fire

Manual effort only reaches so many people, so aim it at a deliberately tiny market you can fully dominate. Total saturation of a small space builds the density needed to spread.

> Like **a fire starting from a concentrated pile of kindling** — spread the same flame across a whole field and it dies; pack it tight and it catches, then it can jump.

```plan
claim: Concentrating limited effort on a deliberately tiny market builds the density needed to catch, where the same effort spread thin dies out.
mechanism: 'Input: a fixed amount of founder effort (heat). Relation: concentrated on a small market it exceeds the ignition threshold and self-sustains; spread across a large market it falls below threshold everywhere and dissipates. Output: saturation of a small space, then it can jump.'
device:
  kind: instrument
  operate: The reader adjusts how wide the market/field the same flame is spread across.
  changes: Spread wide, the flame thins below threshold and dies; packed onto concentrated kindling it crosses the threshold, catches, and then jumps outward.
  resting: A concentrated pile of kindling just beginning to glow at the ignition threshold, a wide unlit field waiting beside it.
aha: The same flame that dies across a whole field catches when packed tight — critical mass is about density, not total size.
data: 'Facebook launched Harvard-only: a potential market of only a few thousand, but a critical mass signed up because it felt made for them; stayed college-by-college for a while after.'
source: Sometimes the right unscalable trick is to focus on a deliberately narrow market. It's like keeping a fire contained at first to get it really hot before adding more logs. That's what Facebook did. At first it was just for Harvard students.
```

# The Idea Is a Vector, Not a Point

A startup isn't just *what you'll build*. It's what you'll build **plus** the unscalable things you'll do to get it started — a direction and a magnitude of effort.

And that early hands-on push isn't wasted scaffolding you throw away. The habits it forms — obsessing over users, learning by contact — often become the company's DNA, permanently, for the better.

```plan
claim: A startup is a vector — what you'll build plus the unscalable effort to start it — and that early push often becomes the company's permanent DNA.
mechanism: 'Composition of two components into one vector: ''what you build'' (direction) added to ''the unscalable things you do'' (magnitude of effort). Relation: the effort component isn''t discarded scaffolding — it persists as habits (obsessing over users, learning by contact) baked into the company for good.'
device:
  kind: figure
  resting: Two arrows composed tip-to-tail into a single resultant vector — 'build' plus 'unscalable effort' — with the effort component annotated as carrying forward into the company's lasting DNA rather than being thrown away.
source: Instead we should try thinking of them as pairs of what you're going to build, plus the unscalable thing(s) you're going to do initially to get the company going. In the best case, both components of the vector contribute to your company's DNA... if you have to work hard to delight users when you only have a handful of them, you'll keep doing it when you have a lot.
```
