An explorable reading · John Vervaeke, “The Metaphysics of Mattering” (2024 talk)

Why anything matters to you

Meaning in life comes down to mattering: being bound to something real that you help shape. Vervaeke argues this binding is also the core of intelligence, and since it can't be reduced to logic or rules, relation itself, not things, may be what is most basic.

meaning-in-life/

Purpose is only one part of a meaningful life, and not the part that counts most

Psychologists now study meaning in life, not the meaning of life. The question isn't a cosmic proposal. It's what keeps you going when you fail, when things feel futile, when people let you down and you let yourself down.

Martela and Steger (2016) drew the research together into three dimensions. Vervaeke notes that our culture likes to shrink meaning down to the first one, purpose. Costin and Vignoles (2020) then gave good evidence for a fourth, mattering, and found that it matters more than purpose.

A later review by Martela and Steger replicated the finding, and showed significance and mattering are two sides of one shared phenomenon. That shared phenomenon is what the rest of the talk is about.

meaning-in-life/mattering/

Mattering means being connected to something real that you make a difference to

Philosopher Susan Wolf, in Meaning in Life and Why It Matters, argues meaning can't be reduced to mastering your environment, or even to morality: she gives examples of people living admirable lives who are very lonely. People say they want to be “connected to something larger than themselves”. But if you're chained to an ocean liner, that isn't it. It's a metaphor, and the work is to unpack it.

Vervaeke turns her answer into three diagnostic questions. All three answered, you have meaning in life. One missing, it's reduced. All missing, you're in trouble. Switch them off one at a time.

Three questions and the 3 / reduced / trouble rule are from the talk. The example in each verdict is our illustration.

This converges with independent research on belonging by Kelly-Ann Allen and others: without a sense of belonging you're in trouble psychologically, physiologically and socially. Belonging, Vervaeke says, is like niche construction in biology. The organism is shaped by its environment, but it also shapes that environment. They take part in one joint process.

He gives this connectedness one word, chosen for its religious overtones: religio, “binding”. Knowing, on this picture, is not a copy of the world in your head. It's contact: mind and world shaping each other.

Mattering isn't about you. The first question is what you want to exist even if you don't.

mattering/autopoiesis/

Things can only matter to something that keeps making itself

Why does anything matter to anyone? Vervaeke borrows from his colleague Evan Thompson: living things are autopoietic, self-making. A tornado is self-organizing too, but it will wander into conditions that destroy it. A paramecium seeks out the conditions that produce, protect and promote its own existence. So it has real needs, and things literally matter to it.

None of that, he says, is in the ontology of standard physics. Flip between the two.

Illustrative. The tornado/paramecium contrast and sucrose as relevant are from the talk; the other items are ours.

You are self-making at every level: your body, your mind (which grows out of the information you take in), your self, and as a mammal and primate, your culture. Because you're always taking care of yourself, you care about this information rather than that. Neuroscientist Read Montague, Vervaeke says, called this the basic difference between you and computers: they don't care about the information they process. You do, because your time and resources are precious.

And because we're social animals, there's a second arrow. Not only “what matters to me?” but “how do I matter to you?” How can I help you become a person? Vervaeke calls this the agapic arrow.

mattering/relevance-realization/

Being intelligent means finding what's relevant in a space too big to search

Spearman found long ago that how you do in maths predicts, well above chance, how you do in sports and in art. That shared factor, general intelligence, is the single best predictor of most of your outcomes. Vervaeke's first premise: what makes you a general problem solver is solving meta-problems, the problems you have to solve while solving any specific problem.

The deepest one is relevance realization: finding the information that matters to you. The trouble is size. Every combination of things you could attend to, remember, consider or do is a possibility, and possibilities multiply. Slide how many things you could notice in a room.

And you're doing it right now, without an algorithm. To be certain what's relevant, you'd have to check all, or most, of the information first. Try to be comprehensively logical like Mr Spock or Data, he says, and you'd commit cognitive suicide trying to get out of bed. Picking things at random doesn't work either.

“Relevance realization is neither algorithmic nor arbitrary.”

This is also his charge against large language models. They don't do relevance realization. They live off ours: we organized their training data, we structure the internet by our judgments of relevance, and we provide the feedback on what's appropriate. They are parasites on us, he says, which doesn't make them harmless.

relevance-realization/no-shortcut/

Every mechanism you might propose for it already needs it

Vervaeke's test: if a process you propose requires relevance realization, it can't be what ultimately generates it. That would be circular. Then he runs the usual candidates through the test.

Start with inference. Take the sentence “it's going to be windy tomorrow”. Its implications are endless. An inference is a selection from them, and which ones you select depends on your plans. Pick tomorrow's plan.

The five plans are from the talk. The implications listed are illustrative, and are 12 of indefinitely many.

The same circle shows up in each of the other candidates.

Representations

A pencil is a writing tool, a stabbing or throwing weapon, the letter I in a mural, something made by humans, post-1960s technology… It has endlessly many true properties. Representing it means picking the aspect that matters (Searle: all representation is aspectual).

Rules

“Be kind.” Kind to a colleague the way you're kind to your partner? To your son the way you're kind to a stranger? Rules for applying rules regress forever. It must end in a skill of judgment (Harold Brown, after Wittgenstein).

Prediction

Going from sample to population risks two errors: missing what's there, and taking a fluke as general. Lowering one raises the other. Which to accept depends on how much a miss or a mistake matters in your context.

Formalization

“24 × 3” is well-defined: you already know what's relevant, and a picture of a platypus isn't an answer. “Go on a successful first date” is ill-defined. Most real problems are, and turning a problem into a formal system is itself one.

Logic, rules and representation all run on relevance realization. None of them can ground it.

Vervaeke frames this against the left hemisphere in Iain McGilchrist's sense, the conference's honoree: it doesn't lack relevance realization, but it can't ground itself. A paper with Johannes Jaeger, Anna Riedl, Alex Djedovic and Denis Walsh makes the formal case: you can't formalize the process of formalization.

relevance-realization/transjective/

Relevance is neither in you nor in the world. It's a relation between them.

Is it relevant? “It depends.” So relevance isn't simply objective. But it isn't arbitrary either, because you can be wrong: you thought she was angry, and she was afraid. Vervaeke coins a word for this: transjective, neither thrown under (subject) nor thrown against (object), but thrown between.

Meaning in life, he proposes, is relevance realization. Both are religio, a binding. If you weren't solving that meta-problem you wouldn't be a cognitive agent at all.

Catherine Pickstock (Aspects of Truth) argues modernity's divisions all broke down the same way: analytic/synthetic (Quine), fact/value (Putnam), theory/data. Is relevance a fact or a value? Both, and neither.

If the way you think and the way the world is organized take part in one relation, then relationality is primordial. Information and intelligibility are relational notions, and we judge what's real by intelligible information.

That clashes with the metaphysics we inherited. James Filler (Neoplatonism, Heidegger, and the History of Being) blames Aristotle: what's ultimately real are substances, individual things, with properties belonging to them the way a predicate belongs to the subject of a sentence. But you can't get relations out of substances. A relation isn't a property of one substance, or it could have it alone. It isn't an action of one, or it could do it alone. Aristotle
Aristotle

Things first

The most real things are individual substances. Properties belong to them. Knowing is conforming: mind and world share form, so they meet.

Nishida's objection

A contradiction

All knowledge is predication. “Red is a colour” puts something into a category. But how can categorical knowledge grasp a thing that is, by definition, an individual?

Medieval scholasticism

Nominalism

Thinkers kept Aristotle's ontology and threw out his epistemology. There are no universals out there. They're only in the mind.

Then

Representation and dualism

If the real patterns are only in the mind, mind and world no longer conform. The mind must hold representations of an outside it can't touch, and becomes a different substance.

Then

Materialism

Dualism hits its own contradictions and collapses. All that exists are individual material objects. The mind becomes one more of them, and nothing matters to anything.

Vervaeke's proposal

Relation first

Invert it, with the Neoplatonists: the relata emerge from the relationality. Mind and world can conform again, bound in religio.

relation/the-sacred/

The sacred is what's most real, most good, and most transforming, and it's a relation

Vervaeke closes with the Canadian philosopher J. L. Schellenberg (an atheist, he notes) and his “triple transcendent”. For something to be sacred it has to be ultimately real, ultimately good, and ultimately transforming. The relational ground, he argues, is all three, and it loops back to where the talk began.

That's the sacred. And that's the metaphysics of mattering.
