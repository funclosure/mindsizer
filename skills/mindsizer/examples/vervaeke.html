Meaning in life

more: meaning in vs of life, the later review

Meaning in life is different from the meaning of life: it isn't a theory of the cosmos. Our culture, Vervaeke says, likes to shrink meaning down to purpose “because it's great for capitalism”.

A later review by Martela and Steger replicated the result, and found that significance and mattering are two aspects of one shared phenomenon. The rest of the talk is about that phenomenon.

Meaning in life›Mattering

Mattering means being connected to something real that you make a difference to

The philosopher Susan Wolf calls it connectedness. The talk turns it into three questions. Answer all three and you have meaning in life; miss one and it's reduced; miss all three and you're in trouble.

The first question is what you want to exist even if you don't.

more: lonely moral lives, belonging, religio

In Meaning in Life and Why It Matters, Wolf argues meaning can't be reduced to mastering your environment, or even to morality: she describes people who live morally admirable lives and are very lonely. “Connected to something larger than yourself” is a metaphor; chaining you to an ocean liner wouldn't do it.

This converges with research on belonging by Kelly-Ann Allen and others: without it you're in trouble psychologically, physiologically and socially. Vervaeke compares belonging to niche construction: the environment shapes the organism and the organism shapes the environment, as a beaver and its pond make each other (our example).

He names this connectedness religio, “binding”, religious overtones intended. Mattering isn't about you in the egocentric sense.

Mattering›Self-making

Things can only matter to something that keeps making itself

A tornado organizes itself, but wanders into conditions that destroy it. A paramecium is autopoietic, self-making: it seeks out what keeps it going. So it has needs, and sucrose matters to it.

flip:

Caring comes from having a self to keep.

more: Thompson, Montague, the agapic arrow

The idea comes from his colleague Evan Thompson. None of it, Vervaeke says, is in the ontology of standard physics.

You are self-making all the way up: as a body, a mind, a self and a member of a culture. Because you're always taking care of yourself, you care about this information rather than that. Neuroscientist Read Montague called this the basic difference between you and computers: computers don't care about the information they process.

As primate mammals we add a second arrow: not only “what matters to me?” but “how do I matter to you?”. He calls it the agapic arrow.

Mattering›Relevance realization

Intelligence is finding what's relevant, and it is neither algorithmic nor arbitrary

What makes you generally intelligent is solving relevance realization: finding, among more possibilities than there are particles in the universe, the few that matter to you. Say you're following a talk. Try three ways to choose what to attend to.

You never solve the room. You realize the small part of it that matters to you.

more: Spearman, Spock, and large language models

Spearman found that how you do in maths predicts how you do in sports and art, far above chance. That shared factor, general intelligence, is the best predictor of most of your outcomes. Vervaeke's premise: being a general problem solver means solving meta-problems, the problems you solve while solving any specific one. (Michael Levin's “light cone” is one.)

Algorithms work by certainty, and to be certain what's relevant you'd have to check everything first. Try to be comprehensively logical like Mr Spock or Data and you'd commit cognitive suicide trying to get out of bed. Picking arbitrarily fails the other way: say “penguin” mid-sentence and everyone asks what the connection is.

“Relevance realization is neither algorithmic nor arbitrary. It's in between.”Vervaeke

The talk also turns this on large language models: they don't do relevance realization, they live off ours. We organized their data, structure the internet by our judgments of relevance, and provide the reinforcement.

more: Cherniak's 138, ill-defined problems, McGilchrist

Christopher Cherniak gave a formal proof that checking just 138 propositions, even at absurd speed, would take the rest of the history of the universe.

Most real problems are ill-defined: taking notes in a lecture, joining a conversation, knowing how close to stand to someone at a funeral. A paper by Jaeger, Riedl, Djedovic, Walsh and Vervaeke makes the formal case: you can't formalize the process of formalization.

Vervaeke frames this with Iain McGilchrist's left hemisphere: it isn't without relevance realization, but it can't ground itself. A framework that treats logic, rules and representation as the whole story relies on the relevance realization it claims to capture.

Relevance realization›Transjective

Relevance is neither in you nor in the world. It's a relation between them.

Is this relevant? “It depends”, so it isn't simply objective. But you can be wrong (you thought she was angry; she was afraid), so it isn't arbitrary either. Vervaeke's word for the middle is transjective.

sub-ject

Thrown under

Only in you. Could never be mistaken.

trans-jective

Thrown between

A real relation. It depends, and you can get it wrong.

ob-ject

Thrown against

Only out there, the same for anyone.

Meaning in life, Vervaeke proposes, is this relevance realization: both are a binding.

more: Pickstock on modernity's divisions

If you weren't solving this meta-problem you wouldn't be a cognitive agent at all, so it couldn't matter more.

Catherine Pickstock (Aspects of Truth) argues that modernity's big divisions all broke down the same way: analytic/synthetic (Quine), fact/value (Putnam), theory/data. Vervaeke asks whether relevance is a fact or a value, and answers: both, and neither.

Relation comes first

A world made of things can't hold relations, so relation must come first

James Filler argues we inherited the wrong picture from Aristotle: reality as individual things with properties. You can't get relations out of things. Scroll through how that picture unravelled.

Aristotle

Things first

The most real things are individual substances with properties. Mind and world share form, so knowing is conforming.

Nishida's objection

A contradiction

All knowledge is by category, yet the most real things are individuals. How can categories grasp them?

medieval scholasticism

Nominalism

Keep Aristotle's things, drop his way of knowing: universals exist only in the mind.

then

Representation and dualism

Mind and world stop conforming. The mind must hold representations, and becomes a different substance.

then

Materialism

Dualism collapses under its contradictions. Only material objects exist; the mind is one more.

Neoplatonism, per Filler

Relation first

Invert it: the things related emerge from the relation. Mind and world can conform again.

Start from things and you lose relation, and with it mattering. Start from relation and mind and world fit together again.

more: Filler's argument, the Cappadocians, the root

Filler's argument (Neoplatonism, Heidegger, and the History of Being: Relation as Ultimate Ground): is a relation a property of one thing? No, then it could have it alone. An action of one? No, then it could do it alone. A connection? That's already a relation.

Vervaeke reaches the same place from the other side: if the grammar of your cognition and the organization of reality take part in one relation, then relationality is primordial. The Neoplatonic inversion was taken up by the Cappadocian Fathers: a Trinity that is inherently relational.

At the root of the unravelling, he says, is treating subject-predicate logic as the shape of reality.

Relation comes first›The sacred

The relational ground is what's most real, most good and most transforming: the sacred

The atheist philosopher J. L. Schellenberg says the sacred must be ultimately real, good and transforming. Vervaeke argues the relational ground passes all three.

Schellenberg's testwhy it passes, per Vervaeke
Ultimately realIt is the ground of being.
Ultimately goodIt makes every judgment of truth, goodness and beauty possible.
Ultimately transformingIt exceeds any attempt to close it with logic and speech, so conforming to it changes you.
“That's the sacred, and that's the metaphysics of mattering.”Vervaeke, closing the talk
